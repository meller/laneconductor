The AI engineer, the software developer role and the Jevons paradox

2026-11-25 · notes from building LaneConductor

There is a fear around AI in engineering, and I hear it in two forms: "AI will take my job", and "I don't do engineering any more, the agent writes the code and I approve it". I think the second one is where a new kind of engineering starts, and I have seen this shape before.

Every wave of automation was said to end a job. I remember it with the cloud (nobody would need to run physical servers), with CI and CD, with Terraform and infrastructure as code (nobody would need to do operations by hand). The word "NoOps" came out of that. A 2012 InfoQ article [Abel Avram, March 2012] already called the label misleading: the change was toward more automation, not toward no operations staff, and the cloud providers themselves employ many operations specialists. Databases are the example I know best: moving data from files into a database did not remove the work, it asked for more specialist knowledge of how to do it, and different kinds of databases came along for different uses. In 2020 a piece about autonomous databases [ITPro, 8 April 2020] said the DBA role was unlikely to be erased, but that its responsibilities would shift, and that a freed workforce is not guaranteed a job. A 2023 survey that HashiCorp commissioned from Forrester Consulting (almost 1,000 practitioners and decision makers) shows the same shape in the cloud era: 43 percent named lack of needed skills as a top factor in avoidable cloud spend, and its advice was to upskill platform teams, not to do without them [HashiCorp, "State of Cloud Strategy Survey 2023", 12 June 2023].

What stayed was the person who runs and tunes the automation. A DBA does not type every query. They read the plan (EXPLAIN), watch capacity, set limits, and keep an audit trail. The work moved from doing the task to making the automation efficient and safe. DevOps and DBA positions still exist today, even though the same title means something quite different from what it meant ten years ago. That is an observation, not data, but it is the shape I expect for the AI engineer: the title stays and the content changes.

There is an economics idea behind this, and it is the optimistic part. When something gets cheaper to do, people often do more of it, and the total grows. It is called the Jevons paradox, after an observation about coal in 1865: more efficient steam engines raised the demand for coal instead of lowering it [Yale Energy History summary of "The Coal Question"]. A later example is the bank teller. James Bessen wrote in 2015 that ATMs cut the tellers needed per urban branch from 20 to 13 between 1988 and 2004, but urban branches grew 43 percent and the work the machine did not take over (talking to customers) became more valuable [IMF Finance & Development, March 2015]. McDonald's is a more recent one, with a caveat: I have no job counts. It put in self-order kiosks and said staff would move to other roles, such as table service [QSR, 26 June 2017], and in 2026 it was reported to be putting more weight on hospitality, with plans to retrain more than 2 million workers worldwide and service counting in franchise reviews, while keeping the kiosks [The Cool Down, citing the Wall Street Journal]. If code gets cheaper to write, I expect more software to be asked for, not less. That is what I see around me: the demand is for more throughput, even though some jobs are clearly replaced. Databases automated how data is stored and structured, and the new tools automate writing code. But being a software engineer was never about writing the code, or punching holes in a card. That was only one technical part of it. The job is also making sure the code fits the use case, the budget, the project goals and the customers; that it is engineered the right way, not over-engineered and not under-engineered; that it is monitored and logged correctly; and that the work is done in an optimal way. If a coding project becomes a machine that creates code by itself, someone still has to keep that machine running well. I expect such machines to compete with each other, and then the human factor will make a difference.

The same idea applies to the energy AI uses. When a cheaper model came out in early 2025, Microsoft's CEO, Satya Nadella, wrote on LinkedIn and X "Jevons paradox strikes again!", meaning, in his words, that as AI gets more efficient its use will skyrocket [GeekWire, Taylor Soper, 27 January 2025]. Energy and AI depend on each other: if we had not improved how we produce and use energy, starting with coal, we would not have AI, and AI now consumes more of that energy. What I see is that we keep consuming more, because progress depends on things becoming cheaper, and the work grows with the consumption. According to the Jevons paradox, for now, the more efficient it gets, the more we use.

What I see in current work is that people are so happy the code is automated that they forget their job is still there. It is a bit like becoming a manager, a DBA or an architect: a lot of developers, junior and senior, were moved up a level without the skills or the knowledge for it, so they get frustrated or feel they are not needed, instead of filling the gap the AI does not fill.

The role is still being written. In 2023 the Latent Space essay "The Rise of the AI Engineer" [source] predicted a new subdiscipline of software engineering, compared it with the SRE, the DevOps engineer and the data engineer, and said the title was the emerging one, not a settled one. I agree it is not settled. I do not know what a good AI engineer's day looks like in five years, and I do not think anyone does yet.

Engineering was never a trade for nostalgia. If you wanted to keep doing things the old way, you would still be punching cards somewhere, and it does not work like that. It is for people who like to explore, to use new things and to invent, and who then go on with the progress. That is my view, and it is why I find this moment interesting and not only worrying.

What I am doing about it. I am looking for tools and habits an AI engineer could use to make the work in a company more efficient, and LaneConductor is one of them: the plan before, the ticks during, the numbers after, and the decisions written down before the run. If you like exploring this, with measurements and not slogans, I would like to hear from you. The source is public on GitHub, free to use and modify: https://github.com/meller/laneconductor
